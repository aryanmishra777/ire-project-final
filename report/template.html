Does LLM Query Rewriting Actually Improve Scholarly Retrieval?

Aryan MishraTeam Solo Retriever (1 member)November 2026

1Project summary

Many search pipelines ask an LLM to rewrite the user's question before they search. The step is usually reported as one part of a bigger system, so its own effect is hard to see, and the extra LLM call is rarely priced. I measured the rewrite step alone on LitSearch, with BM25 and with a dense retriever, and checked the result on real questions from seven students.

Research questionIn scholarly literature search, does LLM query rewriting improve retrieval over the raw question, by how much, under which retriever, and at what token and latency cost?
Hypothesis and verdictRewriting helps BM25 clearly and dense retrieval much less. Partly supported: BM25 gains significantly more for [[n_inter_sig]] of the 4 rewrites, and HyDE helps both retrievers.

2Set-up

597 real literature-search questions over 64,183 ACL and ICLR papers (titles and abstracts). Retrievers: BM25 and E5-large-v2, both baselines in the LitSearch paper. The rewriter is Qwen 3.6 (35B mixture-of-experts) run locally at temperature 0, every call cached. Each question runs in 10 cells: 2 retrievers × (raw question + 4 rewrites: rephrasing, term expansion, a HyDE-style abstract, multi-query with RRF fusion). Comparisons are paired by question: paired bootstrap (10,000 resamples) for the 95% CI, a randomisation test for p, Benjamini–Hochberg correction over the 8 comparisons per metric. The hypothesis is the difference between the BM25 gain and the dense gain.

Baseline check. Against LitSearch's Table 3, E5 is within [[gap_e5]] points on the averaged measures. BM25 runs up to [[gap_bm25_max]] points higher, probably because my BM25 stems words and drops stopwords. So the dense baseline reproduces, and BM25 is a stronger baseline than the paper's, which makes gains harder to get. Raw nDCG@10 is [[ndcg_raw_bm25]] (BM25) and [[ndcg_raw_dense]] (dense).

4Where rewriting fails

I expected the rewrite to drop the one rare term that points to the right paper (a term in fewer than 100 papers). It did so in [[n_lost]] of [[n_ft]] questions, but on average that cost nothing. And of the [[n_big]] questions where BM25 lost half or more of its nDCG, [[n_big_lost]] had dropped a rare term. The big losses come from a rewrite that keeps the topic but loses the exact wording of the gold paper. Averages hide spread: term expansion under BM25 made [[expand_losses]] questions worse although its mean is positive. HyDE costs the most: about [[tok_hyde]] tokens and a p95 delay of [[p95_hyde]] s per query.

5Pilot with real questions small pilot, judged by the researcher

The professor asked for a test with people. Seven students each gave one question from their own work, in their own words. My literature-review tool ran each question twice, with its LLM query-understanding step on and bypassed. The two top-10 lists were pooled and shuffled, and I graded every paper myself, blind to the run. The participants had no time to rate papers, so the relevance grades and the fidelity scores are mine, not theirs. One further question (P01) was my own dry run and stays out of the numbers.

The rewrite raised nDCG@10 by [[s_d]] (95% CI [[[s_ci]]]), far more than the best gain on LitSearch. A likely reason is that, without the rewrite, a long question goes straight to the Crossref and arXiv search engines, which handle long questions badly. That is my reading, not something the pilot proves.

Mean fidelity (how much of the question the rewrite kept, 1 to 5) is [[s_fid]]. Fidelity and gain correlate at ρ = [[s_rho]]: the rewrites that kept less of the question gained more. With [[s_n]] questions and one judge, it is only a lead.

6Limits

One benchmark (NLP and ML papers) and one rewriting model. Most LitSearch questions have one or two gold papers, so a relevant paper outside the gold set counts as a miss. Prompts were not tuned, on purpose. The pilot has [[s_n]] questions and one judge who knows the hypothesis.
